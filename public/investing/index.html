Buy the haystack.

A slow, boring and very old way to grow money: own a little of every company in the world, pay almost nothing for it, and wait.

Two world wars, a pandemic, a depression, oil shocks, bubbles and crashes. Every one of them hurt. None of them stopped it.

    The tool

    One fund. Every company.

    An index is a list: say, every listed company in the world, each counted by its size. An index fund simply buys everything on the list. An ETF is an index fund you buy and sell like a share.

    • It owns everything

      One fund, thousands of companies. If a company grows, you own it. If one fails, it was a tiny slice. Nobody has to guess which is which.

    • Nobody picks

      No star manager, no research team, no trading on hunches. The fund just follows the list, so it costs very little to run: often .

    • You get the market

      You give up any shot at beating the market. In exchange you get its return, minus a tiny fee. That alone beats most professionals once their fees are counted.

    Picking stocks

    Why not just pick the winners?

    Because finding them is much harder than it looks, and paying someone else to try usually costs more than it earns.

    Picking stocks is a game against professionals with more data, more time and faster computers. Before costs, all investors together earn exactly the market’s return. After costs, the average active investor must do worse than the index. And they do: over 15 to 20 years, about nine in ten actively managed US stock funds trail their index.

    What costs cost

    “In investing, you get what you don’t pay for.”

    Fees look tiny: a percent or two a year. But they compound for decades, just like returns, only against you.

    Why weigh by size

    The market does the work

    Prices already hold everyone’s best guess about every company. Weighing by size means you hold the market’s own view, and as prices move your weights move with them. There is nothing to rebalance and almost nothing to trade, which keeps costs down.

    Why the whole world

    Nobody knows the next winner

    In 1900 Britain was the world’s biggest stock market, about a quarter of it. The US was around 15%. Russia’s market was large too, and in 1917 it went to zero. Today the US is nearly two thirds. A world fund follows whoever leads without you having to call it.

    Green investing

    Can you buy just the green part?

    Yes. Funds that do it are sold as “ESG”: environmental, social and governance, or how a company treats the planet, its people, and how it’s run. They take the normal index and leave out the worst. Fine, as long as you know what that does, and what it doesn’t.

    • Leave out the worst

      The lightest version starts from the normal index and drops whole industries: controversial weapons, tobacco, often coal. Most of the market stays in.

    • Keep the best-rated

      A stricter fund keeps only the companies a rating firm scores highest within each industry. Some tilt instead: everyone stays, the high scorers get more of your money.

    • Read past the label

      Since 2025, an EU fund with “ESG” or “sustainable” in its name has to live up to it with at least 80% of its money. Still, a label is a promise, not a measurement.

    What you’d own

    One company in four

    The strictest green version of MSCI’s world index (called ACWI SRI) keeps 585 of its 2,413 companies. Fewer companies means bigger bets on the ones left: Nvidia is 8.0% of the green index against 4.7% of the world index. Energy companies are 0.1% of it.

    The evidence: returns, impact, ratings
    What it does to your return

    Different, not better

    Over the long run, theory says to expect a little less. When many investors shun a stock, its price drops until the people still willing to own it are paid extra for it. Green stocks did well in recent years mostly because worries about the climate grew faster than anyone expected, and a surprise can’t be counted on twice. The flip side: “sin stocks” in alcohol, tobacco and gambling beat comparable companies by about 2.5% a year from 1926 to 2006.

    What it does to the world

    Less than you’d hope

    When you sell a share, someone else buys it. Economists Jonathan Berk and Jules van Binsbergen estimate that all the selling by green and ESG investors so far has raised targeted companies’ by about 0.44 : less than a hundredth of a percent. Moving it by one full percentage point would take 86% of all investors. Another study finds that making money pricier for the dirtiest companies can even make them dirtier. Owning shares and voting them is more likely to change a company than selling them.

    Who decides what’s good

    The raters don’t agree

    Credit ratings from different agencies agree almost perfectly: a correlation of 0.99. ESG ratings from six big raters agree far less: of 0.38 to 0.71. In 2022, S&P dropped Tesla from its S&P 500 ESG index while ExxonMobil stayed in. Elon Musk called ESG “a scam”. A year later Larry Fink, who runs BlackRock, the world’s largest fund manager, stopped using the word, saying it had been weaponized.

    If you want it

    The Bogle way to invest green

    01

    Keep it broad and cheap.

    A world index fund that only leaves out the worst keeps most of the haystack. Compare costs: many green index funds now cost about the same as plain ones.

    02

    Expect the market, give or take.

    Not more. Some years your fund will trail the world index by several points. Hold on anyway; that’s the deal you chose.

    03

    Do good directly.

    Your fund’s exclusions barely move a company. Voting your shares, giving, and changing what you buy and use do more. Investing green is fine for your values, not a way to beat the market or fix it.

    Risk

    How much can you stomach?

    Stocks grow your money; steady it. How you split the two is the one big choice in this plan, and it’s about you, not about the market.

    %
    100% stocks

    Bogle’s rule of thumb: hold roughly your age in bonds, so 30% at 30 and 60% at 60. Ben Felix would add that it’s really about how much you can sit through without selling. The mix matters most around the time you start .

    Rebalancing keeps your mix where you chose it: once a year, sell a little of what grew and buy what fell. It quietly makes you buy low and sell high.

    Notes & sources

    How it’s computed: a mix is rebalanced every year, so 60/40 earns 60% of that year’s stock return plus 40% of the bond return. “Deepest fall” is the biggest drop from a year-end high to a later year-end low. These are unhedged foreign bonds, which swing more than what you’d hold: for the bond part, pick bonds in your own currency or hedged to it.

    The whole plan

    Five rules, then go live your life

    01

    Own the whole world.

    One total world index fund covers it. You don’t need a second one to “diversify” the first.

    02

    Keep costs close to zero.

    Fees are the one part of your return you control. Every tenth of a percent compounds against you for decades.

    03

    Don’t time it.

    Invest when you have money, not when it feels right. Nobody knows when the next crash or rally comes.

    04

    Match the risk to your stomach.

    Stocks can fall by half and stay down for years. If you would sell in a crash, hold some bonds so you won’t. The best plan is the one you can stick to.

    05

    Stay the course.

    Bogle’s phrase. The returns on the chart above went to the people who were still holding after the bad years, not to those who left.

    Getting started

    Start in four steps

    1. Keep a buffer first.

      Put three to six months of expenses in a savings account. Money you need within about five years doesn’t belong in stocks.

    2. Open an account.

      Any with a cheap total-world index fund or ETF will do. Compare the total yearly costs, fund and platform together.

    3. Pick one fund.

      One world stock fund is the whole haystack. Want bonds in it too? A mixed fund holds both and rebalances for you. Vanguard’s LifeStrategy funds come in mixes from 20% to 100% stocks.

    4. Automate it, then look away.

      Set up a monthly transfer and let it run. Got a lump sum? Putting it in at once .
